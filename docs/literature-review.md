# Literature Review: Digital Technologies and Game-Based Learning in Mathematics Education

## 1. Introduction

Mathematics education is an important part of children's cognitive and academic development. Mathematical learning begins with foundational skills such as number recognition, counting, quantity comparison, and understanding relationships between numbers. As children progress through school, these skills develop into more complex competencies, including arithmetic, mathematical reasoning, problem-solving, geometry, and algebra.

Digital technologies have created new opportunities to support mathematics education through interactive exercises, educational games, visual representations, adaptive difficulty, and immediate feedback. However, the effectiveness of digital learning depends on the quality of educational content, the design of the learning experience, the learner's age, and the way the technology is integrated into instruction.

This literature review examines research on digital mathematics applications, educational games, mathematics interventions across educational levels, and the design features that may support learning. It includes evidence from preschool, primary, middle, and secondary education to understand the broader development of digital mathematics education.

The review is particularly relevant to the proposed project, *Development of a Mobile Application for Improving Mathematical Skills in Children*. The overall project considers children aged 4–9, while the initial prototype focuses on children aged 4–5. Its planned activities include number recognition, counting groups of objects, matching quantities to numerals, and guided numeral tracing.

The purpose of this review is to identify evidence-based design principles, critically examine the limitations of previous studies, and explain how the literature informs the proposed application's design.

## 2. Digital Technologies in Mathematics Education

Digital technologies are increasingly used to support mathematics learning across educational levels. They can provide repeated practice, visual explanations, interactive tasks, and feedback that may be difficult to reproduce in conventional learning environments.

Verbruggen, Depaepe, and Torbeyns (2021) conducted a systematic literature review of educational technology in early mathematics education. Their review examined studies using different comparison approaches to evaluate the effectiveness of technology. The authors highlighted that results depend on the intervention and the way technology is implemented. This finding suggests that the presence of technology alone does not guarantee better mathematical outcomes.

Griffith et al. (2020) reviewed 35 studies investigating interactive applications for children younger than six. Their review found evidence of potential learning benefits across early academic skills, particularly mathematics. However, the studies varied considerably in their interventions, outcome measures, and designs, and the risk of bias was unclear in many cases. Consequently, their findings support further investigation of educational apps but do not establish that every application is effective.

A broader perspective is provided by Svane et al. (2023), who systematically reviewed 75 randomized controlled mathematics interventions across educational settings ranging from early childhood education to high school. Most interventions were conducted in elementary school, while fewer targeted high school students and very young children. The review also identified differences in intervention approaches across educational settings.

This research is especially relevant because the proposed application covers a wider age range than its first prototype. It demonstrates why educational approaches should be considered in relation to learners' developmental stages. Activities suitable for a preschool child cannot simply be transferred to an older student without adapting the mathematical content and interaction.

Overall, the literature suggests that digital tools can provide useful opportunities for mathematics practice, but their effectiveness must be evaluated according to specific learning objectives and age groups.

## 3. Educational Mathematics Applications

Educational mathematics applications provide structured activities for developing mathematical skills. They may focus on basic number concepts, arithmetic, geometry, problem-solving, or other mathematical topics.

Outhwaite et al. (2023) examined educational mathematics applications through content analysis and qualitative comparative analysis. Their study evaluated 23 applications previously assessed with children in the first three years of school. Basic number skills were the most common mathematical content, and practice-based applications were the most common type.

The researchers found that learning outcomes were enhanced when applications combined scaffolded learning progression, explanations of correct and incorrect answers, and motivational feedback. This finding is directly relevant to the proposed application. It suggests that the design should not rely only on rewards or attractive visuals. Children should also receive useful feedback and encounter tasks in an appropriate sequence.

Marx et al. (2025) examined 18 digital applications designed to support understanding of part-whole relationships. Their analysis found that none of the reviewed applications provided a systematic progression from hands-on understanding towards more abstract number compositions and decompositions, including number combinations up to ten.

This finding illustrates a limitation in the design of existing applications: an app may provide repeated practice without fully supporting conceptual development. For the proposed project, counting objects and matching quantities to numerals should therefore be designed as related activities rather than isolated exercises.

Papadakis (2021) systematically reviewed tools used to evaluate educational applications for young children. The review is relevant to the assessment of app quality because educational value should be examined through structured criteria rather than visual appearance or popularity alone.

These studies provide a foundation for comparing existing applications and identifying design opportunities. They also suggest that the proposed application should connect its activities to explicit learning objectives and use clear criteria when evaluating its educational design.

## 4. Digital Game-Based Learning and Gamification

Digital game-based learning incorporates educational content into interactive games. Gamification generally refers to the use of game elements, such as points, rewards, progress indicators, and challenges, within a broader activity. Both approaches are relevant to children's mathematics learning, but they should not be treated as equivalent.

DePascale and Ramani (2025) conducted a systematic review of mathematics games for children from preschool through Grade 3. Their review examined the contexts, types, mathematical content, and design features associated with learning. The authors identified a need for further research comparing games across different contexts and examining factors that influence learning.

Kolovou et al. (2026) reviewed 103 empirical studies of digital game-based learning in early childhood and primary mathematics education. The review reported predominantly positive findings for mathematics achievement, particularly in number and operations and geometry. It also identified positive findings related to motivation, attitudes towards mathematics, and collaborative learning.

Although these reviews provide encouraging evidence, they do not show that every game feature improves learning. Their findings cover different games, age groups, mathematical topics, and implementation conditions. Therefore, the proposed application should use game elements to support learning rather than treat engagement as the only measure of success.

Benavides-Varela et al. (2020) conducted a meta-analysis of digital interventions for children experiencing mathematical learning difficulties. Their research is useful because it examines digital interventions from the perspective of learning outcomes rather than assuming that digital or game-based formats are automatically superior. The findings support a careful evaluation of intervention design and the educational purpose of each activity.

For the proposed application, appropriate game elements may include short activities, gradual progression, positive feedback, and rewards for completing tasks. A recurring guide character could help present instructions and connect activities into a coherent experience. These are design proposals informed by the literature, not features whose effectiveness has already been demonstrated in this particular project.

## 5. Mathematics Learning Across Educational Levels

Research on mathematics education covers learners at different developmental stages. This broader evidence is useful for understanding how educational approaches change as mathematical skills become more complex.

Svane et al. (2023) examined mathematics interventions from early childhood education to high school. Their review identified 75 randomized controlled interventions and found that interventions were distributed unevenly across educational settings. Elementary school accounted for the largest proportion, while high-school interventions were comparatively limited.

Darmanova, Abylkassymova, and Nurmukhamedova (2025) reviewed technology use in middle and high school mathematics education. Their review discusses digital tools and learning approaches used with older students. This literature provides a broader perspective on technology-supported mathematics learning, including the importance of selecting tools appropriate to the educational content.

However, findings from middle and high school should not be directly transferred to preschool learning. Older learners may work with algebraic concepts, complex problem-solving, or dynamic mathematical representations, whereas children aged 4–5 require simple instructions, concrete examples, and foundational number activities.

The relevance of this broader research is therefore conceptual rather than direct. It demonstrates that digital mathematics education is a wide field and that applications should be adapted to the target age group and learning objectives.

For the proposed project, the overall age range of 4–9 may eventually require different learning worlds or levels. The initial prototype should remain focused on a smaller set of foundational skills for children aged 4–5. More advanced mathematical content can be considered during later development.

## 6. Guided Play, Feedback, and Learning Progression

The structure of learning activities is an important factor in educational application design. Children may need opportunities to explore independently while also receiving guidance when they encounter difficulty.

Skene et al. (2022) conducted a systematic review and meta-analysis of guided play in educational contexts. Their findings showed advantages for some learning outcomes, including selected early mathematics outcomes, although effects differed between measures and comparison conditions. This suggests that guided play can be useful in some contexts but should not be treated as universally superior to other teaching approaches.

Outhwaite et al. (2023) similarly highlighted the importance of scaffolded learning progression and feedback in mathematics applications. Their findings suggest that learning outcomes may be enhanced when children receive explanations of answers and motivational feedback while progressing through tasks.

Together, these studies support several possible design principles:

- Introduce basic activities before more challenging tasks.
- Provide clear instructions suitable for the child's age.
- Offer helpful feedback when an answer is incorrect.
- Allow children to practise a skill more than once.
- Use encouragement without making rewards more important than learning.
- Connect related activities so that children can move from recognising numerals to understanding quantities.

These principles will inform the proposed prototype. However, they must be implemented and evaluated in the context of early mathematics rather than assumed to guarantee learning improvements.

## 7. Learning Through Numbers, Quantities, and Visual Representations

Foundational mathematics involves more than recognising written numerals. Children also need to understand the relationship between a number symbol and the quantity it represents.

The research reviewed by Verbruggen et al. (2021) and Griffith et al. (2020) supports investigating digital activities for early mathematics learning. Outhwaite et al. (2023) also found that basic number skills, including number representation and relationships, were common targets of evaluated applications.

Marx et al. (2025) further demonstrated that applications differ in how systematically they support conceptual understanding of numerical relationships. Their findings indicate that repeated exercises alone may not provide a complete learning progression.

These findings are relevant to the proposed prototype because it combines several connected tasks:

1. **Number recognition:** children identify a numeral.
2. **Counting objects:** children count a small group of familiar items.
3. **Quantity matching:** children connect a group of objects with the correct numeral.
4. **Guided numeral tracing:** children follow the visible outline of a numeral with their finger.

The first three activities address different aspects of number understanding. Their combination could provide a more coherent learning sequence than treating each skill as unrelated practice. This is a design rationale that must be tested during evaluation.

## 8. Guided Numeral Tracing and Touchscreen Interaction

Guided numeral tracing is a proposed feature intended to help children become familiar with the shapes and directions of numerals. A child could follow a visual outline with a finger and receive simple guidance during the activity.

The reviewed studies provide evidence about educational applications, early mathematics, feedback, and game-based learning. However, the sources reviewed here do not establish that touchscreen tracing necessarily improves handwriting with a pencil.

It is therefore important to distinguish between successful completion of a tracing task and independent numeral formation. These outcomes measure different skills.

The prototype could evaluate whether children can follow the on-screen tracing path, complete the activity, and recognise the numeral afterwards. If the project later aims to demonstrate improvements in pencil-based handwriting, a separate assessment would be required.

This limitation is important because it prevents the project from making unsupported educational claims. Guided tracing should be described as a feature to investigate rather than a proven handwriting intervention.

## 9. Critical Analysis and Research Gap

The reviewed literature provides a strong rationale for investigating digital mathematics applications, but several limitations remain.

First, research findings vary across age groups, applications, mathematical domains, and implementation conditions. A result from an application used by primary-school students cannot automatically establish effectiveness for children aged 4–5.

Second, engagement and educational effectiveness are different outcomes. Games may be enjoyable without necessarily producing measurable improvements in mathematical understanding. Evaluations should therefore consider both usability and learning-related outcomes.

Third, the design of an application matters. Research by Outhwaite et al. (2023) and Marx et al. (2025) indicates that mathematical content, feedback, and learning progression should be examined rather than treating applications as interchangeable products.

Fourth, many reviewed studies evaluate specific interventions rather than the exact combination of features proposed in this project. The reviewed literature does not establish that combining number recognition, counting objects, quantity matching, and guided numeral tracing in one application will necessarily improve mathematical skills.

Finally, the evidence for guided numeral tracing in this specific context remains insufficient in the reviewed sources. Its value should be assessed cautiously and separately from handwriting outcomes.

The project addresses a practical design opportunity by developing a mobile application that connects foundational mathematical activities in one learning experience. The initial prototype will focus on children aged 4–5, while later development may consider additional activities for children aged 6–9.

The main research gap is not the absence of mathematics applications in general. Instead, the project will investigate how a specific combination of foundational number activities, feedback, age-appropriate progression, and guided interaction can be implemented and evaluated within one prototype.

## 10. Design Implications for the Proposed Application

Based on the reviewed literature, the following design priorities have been identified:

1. **Clear learning objectives:** each activity should focus on a specific mathematical skill.
2. **Connected activities:** number recognition, counting, and quantity matching should reinforce one another.
3. **Age-appropriate interaction:** instructions and visual elements should be suitable for children aged 4–5.
4. **Progressive difficulty:** activities should move from simpler tasks towards more challenging ones.
5. **Explanatory feedback:** children should receive understandable feedback when they answer incorrectly.
6. **Motivational design:** rewards and a guide character may support engagement but should not replace mathematical learning.
7. **Purposeful screen use:** activities should be short and focused on learning goals.
8. **Evaluation:** usability and selected learning-related outcomes should be assessed separately.
9. **Future expansion:** activities for older children should be designed according to their mathematical level rather than simply extending preschool tasks.

These priorities will inform the functional requirements, prototype design, and evaluation plan. They represent design implications derived from the literature and should not be interpreted as evidence that the proposed application has already achieved these outcomes.

## 12. Conclusion

The reviewed literature suggests that digital technologies can provide opportunities to support mathematics learning across educational levels. However, their value depends on the mathematical content, the design of activities, the feedback provided, and the suitability of the intervention for the target learners.

Research on mathematics applications highlights the importance of connected learning activities, explanatory feedback, and progressive difficulty. Studies of game-based learning provide a rationale for investigating interactive formats, while broader mathematics intervention research demonstrates the importance of considering differences between educational levels.

For the proposed project, the literature supports investigating an application that combines number recognition, counting familiar objects, matching quantities to numerals, and guided numeral tracing. These activities will be developed for the initial prototype for children aged 4–5, with possible expansion to older age groups in later versions.

The reviewed evidence does not prove that the proposed application will improve mathematical skills. Its educational value must be evaluated through a prototype and an appropriate evaluation plan. This approach allows the project to use existing research responsibly while producing a practical contribution to digital mathematics learning.
